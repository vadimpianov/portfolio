<div class="case-lead">

The company collects and aggregates data from hundreds of sources — historical data, clinical trials by phase, patents, sales and adverse events for drugs from open sources, as well as data from the US regulator FDA and the European EMA. These add up to reports on a drug, a disease and a company.

The reports are used by analysts, BD managers and investors at pharma companies: before betting on a new molecule, they look for historical analogues. The stakes are high: according to <a href="https://www.bio.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">BIO</a>, only 7.9% of drugs entering phase 1 trials reach approval, and the journey takes 10.5 years on average.

Users all said the same thing: “I open a report and don't know where to look.” Every third support request was about the interface rather than the data

## Why did analysts get tired so quickly?

I started by watching people work

<Flow>

I interviewed 12 analysts from five client companies and watched them prepare real reports. Their work demands keeping everything in mind at once: comparing competing drugs, spotting a patent cliff in time, forecasting revenue and assessing clinical trial risk. Product analytics showed the average uninterrupted session lasted 27 minutes.

The problem wasn't the amount of data but the way it was presented. The company gathers information on 1,700+ companies and 9,770+ drugs from hundreds of sources and updates it every day. All of it went into the report as is. The drug report was a single page more than 16,000 pixels tall: nine sections — from sales to safety — and almost 30 tables and charts. All of it was laid out poorly, with the wrong contrast and hard-to-read fonts. Every table had been built on its own, with its own width, font size, row height and header color, and numbers aligned left in one place and centered in another. The eye had to readjust to every block.

To compare two drugs, an analyst scrolled through two such reports and matched the numbers by hand. FDA and EMA data lived in separate tables with different field names, and the revenue, patent and trial charts didn't line up in time. 64% of sessions ended with an export to Excel: analysts assembled their reports where the table columns could at least be lined up

</Flow>

<Scale />

## What we changed

One system for every table and chart

<Flow>

Since we had only just joined the project, nobody knew what the report sections could look like — so I went through more than 150 reports to find and describe what any report is made of and what each section looks like when it is filled to the maximum. Later I built a design system for each of the reports — the design became the knowledge base.

- One grid with generous 120px side margins to create a sense of space in such a dense layout
- To avoid a jagged “Christmas tree”, I had to design a grid specifically for tables. All columns are synchronized
- Links stand out clearly: we aggregate data from third-party sources and have to prove it
- Section tabs stay visible while scrolling so you can jump between sections, and every section collapses so you can tailor the page to yourself
- You can share a link to a specific section — very handy when collaborating with colleagues
- FDA and EMA are a switch in every section, with the same columns in the same order
- Empty, loading and error states live inside each section instead of a blank page

</Flow>

<Flow>

“So what? That's basic hygiene,” you might say. Yes, that's right: I simply brought the user experience up to a basic level of usability.

## Search that thinks like an analyst

Reports, companies, diseases and trials from a single field

<Flow>

Eight out of ten sessions started with search — and it was also the most frustrating part. It only matched the exact drug name, suggested nothing and answered a typo or a synonym with an empty page. Almost every third query led nowhere, and analysts went to Google just to check how a molecule is spelled.

We rebuilt search around how analysts actually think — not by names, but by indications, targets and companies:

- Suggestions appear from the third character: recent queries, popular drugs and exact phrases in quotes
- Matches are grouped by meaning — indications, targets, trade names — with the matched part highlighted
- The “75 of 3,271 reports” counter shows at once how narrow the query is and whether it is worth refining
- From a results row you can add a report to favourites or download it as CSV, JSON, XLSX or PPTX without opening it

</Flow>

## My Reports instead of browser bookmarks

Favourites people come back to every day

<Flow>

Favourites existed, but hardly anyone used them: only 21% of analysts had added a single report. It was a flat list of links with no context, and the star was hidden inside the report. People kept their reports in browser bookmarks, notes and Excel, and getting back to yesterday’s report took almost a minute.

We made My Reports the analyst’s home page:

- The star is in every search row and in the report header — add a report where you found it
- Drugs, companies and diseases are grouped separately, and each card already shows the essentials: trade names, “44 marketed · 1 in development”, “89 companies · 120 drugs”
- Two layouts — cards for an overview and a compact list for those with dozens of reports
- When adding, you can pick a collection or create a new one right away
- Removing asks for confirmation, and the empty page explains how to add the first report
- A guest who taps the star sees the sign-in, and after signing in the report is already in favourites

</Flow>

## Collections: a set of reports in a couple of clicks

From search, from a report and from favourites

<Flow>

Analysts prepare sets of reports for investment committees: “Pfizer drugs 1999–2022”, “everyone working on Parkinson’s disease”. It used to mean 20–30 one-by-one exports stitched together in Excel — 45 minutes on average before every meeting. The only way to send a set to a colleague was a zip file by email.

Collections turned this routine into a couple of clicks:

- “Save search results as collection” sits right under the search field: the whole result list becomes a set
- A report goes into a collection when you add it to favourites, with no extra step
- Collection cards show the number of reports, and inside is a table of reports of different types: drugs and companies together
- Under a report’s title you can see which collections it is already in
- A collection is shared with one link and downloaded as a whole

</Flow>

## Alerts: the data comes to the analyst

Approvals, patents and trials without manual checks

<Flow>

New FDA approvals, patents and clinical trials appear every day, but the only way to learn about them was to open the right report. Analysts set calendar reminders and went through a dozen reports by hand once a week — and on average found out about a competitor’s approval nine days late.

We added subscriptions to changes:

- The Get Alerts bell sits in the report header and in the search row, next to the star
- The first subscription asks only for an email — one field, no form
- The Alerts tab in My Reports gathers all subscriptions to drugs, companies and diseases
- The email is a daily digest, not one email per event, so people actually open it
- You can unsubscribe in one click where you subscribed

</Flow>

## Results

Analysts now work with reports twice as long without a break

<Results caption="Before and after" was="was" note="Product analytics for three months before and after the release; SUS — the same 12 analysts" />

<Flow>

In six months we moved all three report types — drug, disease and company — and search onto the new system. We compared results using product analytics for three months before and after the release, and the SUS usability score on the same 12 analysts we started with.

- Uninterrupted work with a report grew from 18 to 41 minutes
- Analysts find the right metric three times faster
- Excel exports dropped almost threefold
- The SUS usability score rose from 52 to 81

The main result is that analysts stopped fighting the interface and went back to working with data. The design system for data lives separately from the reports: new sections ship faster and look just like the old ones

</Flow>
