The company collects and aggregates data from hundreds of sources — historical data, clinical trials by phase, patents, sales and adverse events for drugs from open sources, as well as data from the US regulator FDA and the European EMA. These add up to reports on a drug, a disease and a company.

The reports are used by analysts, BD managers and investors at pharma companies: before betting on a new molecule, they look for historical analogues. The stakes are high: according to <a href="https://www.bio.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">BIO</a>, only 7.9% of drugs entering phase 1 trials reach approval, and the journey takes 10.5 years on average.

## Why did analysts get tired so quickly?

I started by watching people work

<Flow>

I interviewed 12 analysts from five client companies and watched them prepare real reports. Their work demands keeping everything in mind at once: comparing competing drugs, spotting a patent cliff in time, forecasting revenue and assessing clinical trial risk. Product analytics showed the average uninterrupted session lasted 27 minutes.

The problem wasn't the amount of data but the way it was presented. The company gathers information on 1,700+ companies and 9,770+ drugs from hundreds of sources and updates it every day. All of it went into the report as is. The drug report was a single page more than 16,000 pixels tall: nine sections — from sales to safety — and almost 30 tables and charts. All of it was laid out poorly, with the wrong contrast and hard-to-read fonts. Every table had been built on its own, with its own width, font size, row height and header color, and numbers aligned left in one place and centered in another. The eye had to readjust to every block.

To compare two drugs, an analyst scrolled through two such reports and matched the numbers by hand. FDA and EMA data lived in separate tables with different field names, and the revenue, patent and trial charts didn't line up in time. 64% of sessions ended with an export to Excel: analysts assembled their reports where the table columns could at least be lined up.

</Flow>

<Scale />

## What we changed

One system for every table and chart

<Flow>

Instead of redrawing pages one by one, I started with rules. We described what any report is made of — a header, section tabs, a collapsible section, a table, a chart, a tooltip, an empty state — and built a design system for data out of it. Now developers assemble a new section from ready-made components instead of inventing it from scratch.

- One grid: all tables on a page share the same width, row height and font size
- Numbers are right-aligned in tabular figures, text is left-aligned
- Section tabs stay visible while scrolling, sections collapse
- FDA and EMA are a switch in every section, with the same columns in the same order
- Every chart has a table view, every table has sorting and filters
- Empty, loading and error states live inside each section instead of a blank page

The report is as detailed as before: we didn't remove a single number. What changed is how the eye moves through it.

</Flow>

## Charts that read at a glance

One visual language for financials, patents and research

<Flow>

We built every chart on the same rules: the legend on top, axis labels in one size, the period switch always in the same place, a tooltip with the exact value on hover and a "Table view" button for when you need the numbers rather than the shape of the line. Color encodes meaning only — the data source, patent status or trial phase — not decoration.

A company report starts with financials: the share price can be compared with industry indices, revenue is broken down by quarter and by drug, and patents sit on one timeline showing what has expired and what is still protected. An analyst can see when a company's exclusivity ends and what will replace it.

</Flow>

## FDA and EMA in one coordinate system

A source switch instead of two different tables

<Flow>

American and European statistics are structured differently: the FDA has NDA and BLA applications, the EMA has the centralised procedure and its own statuses, and dates, phase names and formats differ. Together with a data analyst we built a mapping dictionary and brought both sources to a common form. Now the source switches with one click in any report section, while columns and row order stay put.

What matters most to an analyst is the probability of success at each stage. It used to be calculated by hand in Excel; now it is right in the disease report: on the competitive landscape map when hovering over any disease, and in the trials section as a chain from phase 1 to approval.

</Flow>

## Tables that answer the question

Sorting and filters instead of scrolling and Ctrl+F

<Flow>

Analysts used to find the row they needed by scrolling and using the browser search. We added sorting by any column, filters by therapeutic area, phase and status, tooltips on headers and pagination instead of an endless sheet. Companies and drugs in the tables link to their reports, so you can jump from a row straight to the details.

</Flow>

## Results

Analysts now work with reports twice as long without a break

<Results caption="Before and after" was="was" note="Product analytics for three months before and after the release; SUS — the same 12 analysts" />

<Flow>

In six months we moved all three report types — drug, disease and company — and search onto the new system. We compared results using product analytics for three months before and after the release, and the SUS usability score on the same 12 analysts we started with.

- Uninterrupted work with a report grew from 18 to 41 minutes
- Analysts find the metric they need three times faster
- Excel exports dropped almost threefold
- The SUS usability score rose from 52 to 81

The main result is that analysts stopped fighting the interface and went back to working with data. The design system for data lives separately from the reports: new sections ship faster and look just like the old ones.

</Flow>
